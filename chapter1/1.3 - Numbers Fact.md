# 1.3 Number Facts

## Learning objectives

By the end of this section, you should be able to:
- classify natural numbers, integers, prime numbers and composite numbers;
- identify factors, multiples, common factors and common multiples;
- calculate the highest common factor (HCF) and lowest common multiple (LCM);
- express integers as products of prime factors;
- recognise square and cube numbers;
- find reciprocals of non-zero numbers;
- distinguish rational from irrational numbers.

---

## 1.3.1 Numbers Visualization: Natural Numbers vs. Integers

> **The Big Idea:** Think of numbers as layers. **Natural numbers** are your basic counting blocks. **Integers** expand that neighborhood to include zero and the negative underworld.

### Quick Breakdown

| Category | What it includes | Example | Rule to remember |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Natural Numbers** ( $\mathbb{N}$ ) | Positive counting numbers | $1, 2, 3, 4, \dots$ | **Starts at $1$.** Zero is not included here. |
| **Integers** ( $\mathbb{Z}$ ) | Whole numbers (positive, negative, & zero) | $ \dots, -2, -1, 0, 1, 2, \dots $ | No decimals or fractions allowed! |

## 1.3.2 Prime vs. Composite Numbers 

> **The Big Idea:** Every number greater than $1$ falls into one of two camps: **Prime** (exclusive club, only divisible by $1$ and itself) or **Composite** (made by multiplying smaller primes together).

A **prime number** is an integer greater than 1 with exactly two positive factors: 1 and itself. Examples are 2, 3, 5, 7, 11 and 13.

A **composite number** is an integer greater than 1 with more than two positive factors. For example, 12 is composite because its factors are 1, 2, 3, 4, 6 and 12.

* **Negative Numbers & Zero:** Excluded from both prime and composite definitions.

## 🔢 1.3.2 Factors vs. Multiples

> **The Big Idea:** 
> * **Factors** are the smaller whole numbers that **fit inside** a target number (they divide it evenly).
> * **Multiples** are the larger numbers you get by **multiplying** your target number by $1, 2, 3, 4, \dots$

---

### 💡 Quick Comparison

| Feature | Factors | Multiples |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **What are they?** | Numbers that divide a target exactly | Products made by multiplying a target |
| **How many exist?** | **Finite** (a limited, fixed count) | **Infinite** (goes on forever $\dots$) |
| **Example for $12$** | $1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12$ | $12, 24, 36, 48, 60, \dots$ |

---

$$1 \times 36 = 36 \quad \mid \quad 2 \times 18 = 36 \quad \mid \quad 3 \times 12 = 36 \quad \mid \quad 4 \times 9 = 36 \quad \mid \quad 6 \times 6 = 36$$

#### Visual Factor Tree
```text
           36
          /  \
         4    9
        / \  / \
       2   2 3  3   -->  36 = 2 × 2 × 3 × 3 (Prime Factorization)
```
All factors of $36$

$$
\boxed{1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 9, 12, 18, 36}
$$

**Worked example 2: Finding factors**

Find all positive factors of $40$.

**Solution**

List whole-number factor pairs that multiply to give $40$

$$ 1 \times 40 = 40, \quad 2 \times 20 = 40, \quad 4 \times 10 = 40,\quad 5 \times 8 = 40 $$

$$
\boxed{1, 2, 4, 5, 8, 10, 20, 40}
$$

**Worked example 3: Finding multiples**

Write the first six positive multiples of $8$.

**Solution**

Multiply $8$ by $1, 2, 3, 4, 5,$ and $6$

$$8 \times 1 = 8, \quad 8 \times 2 = 16, \quad 8 \times 3 = 24, \quad 8 \times 4 = 32, \quad 8 \times 5 = 40, \quad 8 \times 6 = 48$$

Answer

$$
\boxed{8, 16, 24, 32, 40, 48}
$$

---
> [!WARNING]
> **Common Mistake: Don't confuse Factors with Multiples!**
> 
> * **$6$ is a factor** of $30$ because $30 \div 6 = 5$ (it divides evenly with no remainder).
> * **$90$ is a multiple** of $30$ because $30 \times 3 = 90$ (it is created by multiplying).

## 1.3.3 Common factors and common multiples

A **common factor** divides each of the numbers exactly. A **common multiple** is a multiple of every number in the group.

For example:
- Factors of 16: \(1,2,4,8,16\)
- Factors of 24: \(1,2,3,4,6,8,12,24\)

Their common factors are \(1,2,4,8\).

The first positive multiples of 4 are \(4,8,12,16,20,24,\ldots\). The first positive multiples of 6 are \(6,12,18,24,30,\ldots\). Common multiples include \(12,24,36,\ldots\).

### Worked example 4

Find the common factors of 30 and 42.

Factors of 30: \(1,2,3,5,6,10,15,30\).  
Factors of 42: \(1,2,3,6,7,14,21,42\).

The common factors are
\[
\boxed{1,2,3,6}
\]

## 1.3.4 Highest common factor (HCF) and lowest common multiple (LCM)

The **highest common factor (HCF)** is the greatest positive integer that divides all the given numbers exactly. The **lowest common multiple (LCM)** is the smallest positive integer that is a multiple of all the given numbers.

### Method 1: Listing factors and multiples

### Worked example 5

Find the HCF and LCM of 8 and 12.

Factors of 8: \(1,2,4,8\).  
Factors of 12: \(1,2,3,4,6,12\).

The greatest common factor is 4, so
\[
\operatorname{HCF}(8,12)=\boxed{4}.
\]

Multiples of 8 include \(8,16,24,32,\ldots\). Multiples of 12 include \(12,24,36,\ldots\). The smallest positive common multiple is 24:
\[
\operatorname{LCM}(8,12)=\boxed{24}.
\]

### Method 2: Prime factorisation

For the HCF, use the prime factors shared by all the numbers, with the **smallest power** of each shared prime. For the LCM, include every prime factor needed, with the **largest power** of each prime.

### Worked example 6

Find the HCF and LCM of 36 and 60.

\[
36=2^2\times3^2,\qquad 60=2^2\times3\times5
\]

The shared prime factors with the smallest powers give
\[
\operatorname{HCF}=2^2\times3=\boxed{12}.
\]

Using the largest powers of all primes gives
\[
\operatorname{LCM}=2^2\times3^2\times5=\boxed{180}.
\]

**Check:** The HCF must divide both original numbers. The LCM must be divisible by both.

### Worked example 7: Applying HCF

A club has 24 red counters and 40 blue counters. The counters are to be divided into identical groups with no counters left over. What is the greatest possible number of groups?

\[
24=2^3\times3,\qquad40=2^3\times5
\]
The HCF is \(2^3=8\), so there can be \(\boxed{8\text{ groups}}\). Each group contains 3 red counters and 5 blue counters.

HCF is useful for forming the greatest number of equal groups. LCM is useful when repeating events must occur together.

## 1.3.5 Prime factorisation

**Prime factorisation** expresses an integer greater than 1 as a product of prime numbers. For example,
\[
42=2\times3\times7.
\]

### Factor trees

Break a number into factor pairs until every final factor is prime. The order of the factors may differ, but the prime factorisation is unique apart from order.

### Worked example 8

Express 72 as a product of prime factors.

\[
72=8\times9=(2\times2\times2)\times(3\times3)
\]
Therefore,
\[
\boxed{72=2^3\times3^2}
\]

Another method is repeated division by prime numbers. For example,
\[
84\div2=42,\quad42\div2=21,\quad21\div3=7,\quad7\div7=1
\]
so
\[
\boxed{84=2^2\times3\times7}.
\]

Prime factorisation is useful for finding HCF and LCM and for expressing repeated multiplication using powers.

## 1.3.6 Square numbers and cube numbers

A **square number** is the product of an integer multiplied by itself:
\[
6^2=6\times6=36.
\]
The first ten positive square numbers are
\[
1,4,9,16,25,36,49,64,81,100.
\]

A **cube number** is the product of an integer multiplied by itself three times:
\[
4^3=4\times4\times4=64.
\]
The first six positive cube numbers are
\[
1,8,27,64,125,216.
\]

The notation \(n^2\) means \(n\times n\); \(n^3\) means \(n\times n\times n\).

### Worked example 9

Is 81 a square number or a cube number?

Since \(81=9^2\), it is a square number. No integer cubed equals 81, so it is not a cube number.

### Worked example 10

Find the positive integer \(n\) if \(n^2=169\).

\[
n=\sqrt{169}=\boxed{13}
\]
If a question asks for all integer solutions, both \(13\) and \(-13\) satisfy \(n^2=169\).

## 1.3.7 Reciprocals

The **reciprocal** of a non-zero number \(a\) is \(\frac1a\). A number multiplied by its reciprocal equals 1.

Examples:
\[
5\times\frac15=1,\qquad
\frac34\times\frac43=1,\qquad
(-8)\times\left(-\frac18\right)=1.
\]

For a fraction \(\frac ab\), where \(a\ne0\) and \(b\ne0\), its reciprocal is \(\frac ba\). Interchange numerator and denominator and keep the sign.

For decimals, convert to a fraction first if that helps. For example,
\[
0.2=\frac15\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\text{reciprocal}=5.
\]

**Important:** Zero has no reciprocal because division by zero is undefined.

### Worked example 11

Find the reciprocal of 12, \(-\frac25\) and 1.25.

- Reciprocal of 12: \(\boxed{\frac1{12}}\)
- Reciprocal of \(-\frac25\): \(\boxed{-\frac52}\)
- Since \(1.25=\frac54\), its reciprocal is \(\boxed{\frac45}\).

## 1.3.8 Rational and irrational numbers

A **rational number** can be written exactly as
\[
\frac ab
\]
where \(a\) and \(b\) are integers and \(b\ne0\).

Examples include \(\frac34\), \(-5\), \(0.25\) and \(0.333\ldots\).

Every integer is rational because it can be written over 1. A terminating decimal is rational: \(0.25=\frac14\). A recurring decimal is also rational: \(0.333\ldots=\frac13\).

An **irrational number** cannot be written as a ratio of two integers with a non-zero denominator. Examples include \(\sqrt2\), \(\sqrt3\) and \(\pi\). Their decimal expansions do not terminate or repeat in a fixed recurring pattern.

For a positive integer, its square root is rational if the integer is a perfect square. For example, \(\sqrt{49}=7\). If the integer is not a perfect square, its square root is irrational; for example, \(\sqrt{10}\).

A calculator may show a rounded decimal approximation, not the exact value. Write
\[
\sqrt{11}\approx3.3166
\]
rather than claiming that the displayed decimal is exact.

### Worked example 12

Classify each number as rational or irrational.

- \(\frac79\) is rational because it is a ratio of integers.
- \(\sqrt{64}=8\) is rational.
- \(\sqrt{11}\) is irrational because 11 is not a perfect square.
- \(0.125=\frac18\) is rational.
- \(\pi\) is irrational.

## Summary

- Natural numbers are counting numbers starting at 1 under the convention used here.
- Integers include negative whole numbers, zero and positive whole numbers.
- Prime numbers have exactly two positive factors; composite numbers have more than two.
- Factors divide a number exactly; multiples are produced by multiplication.
- HCF is the greatest common factor; LCM is the smallest positive common multiple.
- Prime factorisation expresses an integer as a product of prime numbers.
- Square numbers have the form \(n^2\); cube numbers have the form \(n^3\).
- The reciprocal of a non-zero number \(a\) is \(\frac1a\).
- Rational numbers can be expressed as a ratio of integers; irrational numbers cannot.
